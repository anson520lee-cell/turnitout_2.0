Large language models have achieved remarkable performance on a wide range of natural language processing tasks, yet their ability to reason over long documents remains limited. In this paper, we propose a hierarchical attention framework that segments input documents into semantically coherent chunks and aggregates their representations through a learned routing mechanism. Our approach reduces the computational cost of self-attention from quadratic to near-linear in document length while preserving the ability to capture long-range dependencies. We evaluate the proposed method on five benchmark datasets covering question answering, summarization, and document classification. Experimental results demonstrate that our model consistently outperforms strong baselines, achieving an average improvement of 3.2 points in accuracy. Furthermore, ablation studies reveal that the routing mechanism contributes substantially to these gains. We release our code and trained models to facilitate future research.

Sentiment analysis in low-resource languages remains a challenging problem due to the scarcity of annotated data. In this work, we investigate cross-lingual transfer learning as a means of addressing this limitation. Specifically, we fine-tune a multilingual pretrained encoder on English sentiment data and evaluate its zero-shot performance on twelve typologically diverse target languages. Our analysis shows that transfer effectiveness is strongly correlated with linguistic similarity to the source language and with the amount of pretraining data available for the target. To mitigate performance gaps, we introduce a lightweight adapter-based approach that requires only a few hundred labeled examples in the target language. Extensive experiments confirm that our method yields significant improvements over standard fine-tuning, particularly for languages with limited resources. These findings provide practical guidance for building sentiment classifiers in under-represented languages.

Named entity recognition is a fundamental task in information extraction, with applications ranging from knowledge base construction to question answering. Despite recent progress, existing models often struggle to generalize to entity types not seen during training. We present a novel prompt-based framework that reformulates named entity recognition as a conditional generation problem, enabling the model to leverage natural language descriptions of entity types. By incorporating type definitions directly into the input, our approach supports flexible zero-shot and few-shot recognition without architectural modifications. We conduct comprehensive evaluations on eight datasets spanning biomedical, legal, and news domains. The results indicate that our framework achieves state-of-the-art performance in few-shot settings and remains competitive in fully supervised scenarios. Qualitative analysis further shows that the model effectively exploits semantic information in type descriptions.

Machine translation systems have improved dramatically with the advent of neural architectures, but they continue to exhibit systematic errors when translating idiomatic expressions. This paper presents a comprehensive study of idiom translation across six language pairs. We construct a new evaluation benchmark containing over four thousand idiomatic sentences annotated by professional translators. Using this benchmark, we analyze the behavior of several state-of-the-art systems and identify common failure modes, including literal translation and omission of figurative meaning. Based on these insights, we propose a data augmentation strategy that increases the exposure of the model to idiomatic usage during training. Our experiments show that this strategy improves idiom translation quality by up to 12 percent without degrading performance on general text. We hope that our benchmark and findings will encourage further research on figurative language in translation.

Dialogue systems are increasingly deployed in customer service, healthcare, and education, yet maintaining coherent and engaging conversations over many turns remains an open challenge. We introduce a memory-augmented dialogue model that stores and retrieves salient information from previous turns using a differentiable external memory. The memory is updated dynamically as the conversation progresses, allowing the system to track user preferences and resolve long-distance references. We evaluate our model on three multi-turn dialogue benchmarks using both automatic metrics and human judgments. Results show that the proposed approach generates more consistent and informative responses than competitive baselines. In addition, a detailed error analysis highlights remaining limitations, such as difficulty in handling contradictory user statements. Our findings suggest that explicit memory mechanisms are a promising direction for building more reliable conversational agents.

Recent advances in contrastive learning have led to significant improvements in sentence representation quality. However, most existing methods rely on carefully designed data augmentation strategies that may introduce noise or alter the meaning of the original sentence. In this paper, we propose a simple yet effective approach that uses dropout as the only source of augmentation. We show that passing the same sentence through the encoder twice with different dropout masks produces positive pairs that are sufficiently diverse for effective contrastive training. Experiments on semantic textual similarity and transfer tasks demonstrate that our method achieves performance comparable to or better than more complex alternatives. Moreover, our analysis reveals that the approach improves both alignment and uniformity of the learned embedding space. These results suggest that minimal augmentation can be surprisingly powerful.

Automatic speech recognition performance degrades substantially in noisy and reverberant environments. To address this problem, we propose a multi-stage enhancement pipeline that combines spectral masking with a transformer-based refinement network. The first stage estimates a coarse mask to suppress stationary noise, while the second stage models temporal dependencies to recover fine-grained speech details. We train the entire system end-to-end using a combination of reconstruction and recognition losses. Evaluation on the CHiME-4 and REVERB challenge datasets shows that our approach reduces word error rate by 18 percent relative to a strong baseline. We further demonstrate that the enhancement front-end generalizes well to unseen noise types. Our results highlight the benefits of jointly optimizing enhancement and recognition objectives in challenging acoustic conditions.

Text simplification aims to make complex text accessible to a broader audience, including children, second-language learners, and people with cognitive disabilities. Existing approaches typically treat simplification as a monolingual translation task and require large parallel corpora, which are costly to obtain. We propose an unsupervised method that leverages readability-guided editing to iteratively transform complex sentences into simpler ones. A pretrained language model scores candidate edits for fluency and meaning preservation, while a readability estimator guides the search toward simpler output. Experiments on two standard datasets show that our method produces simplifications that are competitive with supervised systems according to both automatic metrics and human evaluation. We also provide an analysis of the types of edits performed and discuss implications for future work.

Knowledge graph completion seeks to infer missing facts from existing triples and plays a vital role in many downstream applications. Conventional embedding-based methods often ignore the textual descriptions associated with entities and relations. In this paper, we present a unified framework that jointly learns structural and textual representations through a shared transformer encoder. By conditioning on both graph neighborhood and entity descriptions, the model captures complementary information that improves prediction accuracy. We evaluate our approach on four widely used benchmarks and observe consistent gains over state-of-the-art baselines, particularly for entities with sparse connections. Further experiments demonstrate that the model is robust to noisy descriptions and scales efficiently to large graphs. Our work underscores the importance of integrating multiple sources of information for knowledge graph reasoning.

Fake news detection has become an urgent research topic given the rapid spread of misinformation on social media. Most existing detectors rely on content features alone and therefore fail to exploit the social context in which news is shared. We propose a graph neural network that models the propagation patterns of news articles together with user engagement signals. The model learns to distinguish credible from misleading content by aggregating information from the structure of the sharing cascade. Experiments on two real-world datasets show that our approach outperforms content-only and propagation-only baselines by a considerable margin. Additionally, we find that early propagation features are sufficient for accurate detection within hours of publication. These findings have practical implications for the design of automated fact-checking systems.

Pretrained language models exhibit strong performance but are often too large for deployment on resource-constrained devices. Knowledge distillation offers a promising solution by transferring knowledge from a large teacher model to a smaller student. In this work, we investigate how the choice of intermediate representations affects distillation quality. We introduce a layer-matching strategy that automatically selects the most informative teacher layers for each student layer during training. Our experiments on the GLUE benchmark show that the distilled student retains 97 percent of the teacher's performance while being six times smaller and four times faster at inference. We also conduct extensive ablations to understand the contribution of each component. The proposed approach provides an efficient and practical way to compress large language models.

Understanding how bilingual speakers switch between languages within a single utterance is an important question in computational sociolinguistics. We study code-switching in social media text using a corpus of Cantonese-English posts collected from public forums. Through quantitative analysis, we identify syntactic positions at which switches are most likely to occur and examine how switching behavior varies with topic and formality. We then develop a classifier that predicts switch points with an F1 score of 0.81, substantially outperforming a rule-based baseline. Our findings suggest that code-switching follows systematic patterns that can be modeled computationally. We discuss the implications of these results for multilingual language technology and outline directions for future research.
