Urban gardening is growing in popularity across major cities, and for good reason. Rooftop plots and community gardens turn unused space into sources of fresh food, helping residents reduce grocery costs while strengthening local ties. They also cool neighbourhoods, absorb rainwater, and offer pollinators a place to feed.

Still, urban gardens face obstacles. Soil in cities can be contaminated, so testing is necessary before planting anything edible. Land is often temporary, leased on a short-term basis until developers want it back. Volunteers carry most of the work, and enthusiasm can fade when the first harvest is over.

Cities can help by protecting garden sites, funding soil tests, and offering workshops for beginners. With that support, small plots can become lasting community assets rather than short-lived projects.

Many students believe that cramming the night before an exam is an efficient use of time. Research on memory suggests otherwise. Information learned in a single long session fades quickly, while material reviewed in shorter sessions spread over several days is retained far longer. This effect, known as the spacing effect, is one of the most reliable findings in cognitive psychology.

Active recall strengthens it further. Instead of rereading notes, students who test themselves on the material form stronger memory traces. Writing practice questions, using flashcards, or explaining a concept aloud all count as recall.

The practical lesson is simple: begin earlier, study in short bursts, and quiz yourself often. It feels harder than rereading, and that difficulty is exactly why it works.

Plastic pollution has become a visible symbol of environmental neglect. Beaches in Southeast Asia are littered with bottles and bags, and microplastics have been found in rivers, fish, and even drinking water. The material is cheap and convenient, which makes it hard to give up.

Solutions exist at several levels. Individuals can carry reusable bottles and bags, but real change depends on policy. Bans on single-use items, deposit schemes for bottles, and producer responsibility laws have reduced waste in places that adopted them. Companies, for their part, can redesign packaging to use less material or to be easier to recycle.

No single measure will solve the problem, yet together they can shrink it. The sooner governments and businesses commit, the less cleanup future generations will inherit.

Should universities abolish standardized admission tests? Supporters say such tests favour students who can afford coaching and fail to capture creativity or perseverance. Several leading universities have already made test scores optional, reporting that applicant pools became more diverse.

Opponents reply that tests provide a common yardstick in a world where grades vary widely between schools. Without them, admission officers may rely more on essays and interviews, which can also reflect advantage. Some studies even suggest that test scores help identify talented students from under-resourced schools who would otherwise be overlooked.

The debate is unlikely to end soon. A reasonable compromise may be to treat test results as one factor among many and to give schools clear guidance on weighing them fairly.

Artificial intelligence is reshaping healthcare in ways that were hard to imagine a decade ago. Algorithms can now read medical images, flag early signs of disease, and help doctors choose between treatments. In busy hospitals, these tools can reduce waiting times and catch problems that a tired clinician might miss.

At the same time, there are serious questions to answer. If an algorithm makes a mistake, who is responsible? Training data often under-represents certain groups, which can lead to less accurate results for them. Patients also deserve to know when a machine has played a role in their care.

Healthcare systems should therefore adopt AI carefully. Testing across diverse populations, clear accountability, and human oversight must come first. Used wisely, AI can strengthen doctors' judgement rather than replace it.

Cultural festivals do more than entertain; they preserve identity. Hong Kong's Mid-Autumn Festival, with its lanterns, mooncakes, and family dinners, gives each generation a shared reference point. Even young people who rarely follow other traditions often join the celebrations.

Festivals also support local economies. Lantern makers, bakeries, and street vendors depend on seasonal demand, and tourists add to the crowds. Yet commercialization can dilute meaning when events become more about sales than about connection. Plastic decorations and mass-produced gifts have replaced handmade items in many places.

Balancing authenticity with practicality is the central challenge. Communities that involve younger people in planning, and that support craftspeople directly, are more likely to keep traditions alive in forms that still feel genuine.

Time management is a skill that decides how well students cope with the demands of school. Many struggle not because they lack ability but because they lack a plan. Deadlines pile up, and last-minute rushing leads to stress and lower-quality work.

A good approach begins with listing every task and estimating how long each will take. Prioritizing by deadline and importance helps decide what to do first. Breaking large assignments into smaller steps makes them less intimidating and provides a sense of progress. Leaving time for rest is just as important, since tired minds work slowly.

With practice, these habits become automatic. Students who manage their time well tend to feel more in control, perform better, and have more time for the activities they enjoy.

The gig economy offers flexibility that traditional jobs rarely match. Drivers, delivery riders, and freelancers choose when and where to work, and platforms make it easy to find customers. For students and people with caring duties, this freedom can be valuable.

The trade-offs are real, though. Gig workers typically have no paid leave, no pension contributions, and uncertain income from week to week. Algorithms can set pay rates and penalize workers without clear explanation. Disputes over whether such workers are employees or contractors have reached courts in many countries.

Policymakers face a difficult task. They must protect workers without destroying the flexibility that makes gig work attractive. Minimum earnings guarantees, portable benefits, and transparent rating systems are some of the options under discussion.

Water scarcity affects more than two billion people worldwide, and the number is expected to grow. Climate change is altering rainfall patterns, while rising populations and expanding agriculture place greater demands on rivers and aquifers. In some regions, groundwater is being pumped faster than it can recharge.

Technology can help. Drip irrigation uses a fraction of the water needed for flooding fields, and desalination plants turn seawater into drinking water, though at high energy cost. Recycling wastewater is another promising path, already widely used in Singapore.

Technology alone is not enough. Fair pricing, protection of watersheds, and cooperation between countries that share rivers are essential. Treating water as a precious, shared resource is the first step toward making supplies last.

Learning a second language changes how the brain works. Studies show that bilingual people often switch between tasks more easily and may delay the onset of dementia by several years. Beyond these cognitive advantages, a new language opens doors to other cultures, careers, and friendships.

Adults can learn languages successfully, even if children pick up accents more naturally. Consistent practice matters more than talent. Short daily sessions, conversation with native speakers, and exposure to films and music all help. Mistakes are part of the process, and fear of making them is the biggest barrier.

Schools should therefore start language instruction early and make it enjoyable. The reward is not only a new skill but a broader view of the world.
